How this map was made

Delhi Heat Gap shows which Delhi neighbourhoods get hottest, who lives there, and what their streets look like. Everything below is open data, processed once, offline, and published as static files.

What the numbers mean

The city is divided into H3 hexagons at resolution 9, each about 0.1 km². That is small enough to show neighbourhoods and big enough to hide individual homes. Headline numbers use built-up hexagons only, leaving out the Yamuna, the ridge forest and farmland.

Heat gap
Median daytime surface temperature of the hottest 10% of hexagons minus that of the coolest 10%.
People in the heat
Modelled population summed over the hottest 10% of hexagons, rounded to the nearest 10,000.
Night retention
Night surface temperature of a hexagon minus the median for built-up Delhi.
Shade gap
Average share of the street view filled by trees and plants on cool streets, minus hot streets, across every street with enough photos.

Processing

  1. Landsat surface temperature is converted to °C (Kelvin = DN × 0.00341802 + 149.0, minus 273.15). Clouds, cloud shadow and cirrus are masked with the QA_PIXEL band, scenes with under half of Delhi clear are dropped, and the per-pixel median is taken across all remaining April–June days.
  2. Each 30 m pixel is assigned to the hexagon containing its centre and the median is taken. Night data, which is coarser than a hexagon, is sampled at each hexagon’s centre.
  3. Population pixels are summed per hexagon. Age shares from 2020 are applied to the newer totals so that age groups never exceed the total.
  4. A hexagon counts as built-up if at least 15% of it is built-up land or about 500 people live there, and it is less than half water.
  5. As a sanity check, the hottest hexagons are compared with thermal hotspots named in the Heat Wave Action Plan 2026.
  6. For streets, up to 30 recent photos per hot and cool hexagon are segmented into trees and plants, sky, buildings and road. A person checked 50 random results by hand. Only road or skyline photos with no people or recognisable homes are ever shown.

What the data cannot show

  • Surface is not air. Satellites measure how hot roofs and roads get around 10:30 am, not the air people breathe or the heat inside a tin-roofed room at 2 am. Indoor and night heat often hurt most. Weather stations such as Ayanagar measure air temperature, which is why a place can be an official hotspot without topping this map.
  • Night data is coarse. At 1 km, the night chapter shows broad patterns, not streets.
  • Population is modelled, not counted. WorldPop spreads census totals using satellite cues, and the last ward-level census is from 2011. We always say “about” and round.
  • Street photos may be old. Each comparison shows its photos’ capture years; photos older than about five years are skipped.
  • Correlation is not proof. “Less shade, more heat” is a pattern across many streets, not a verdict on any one street.

How we try to avoid harm

  • We name causes, not people: “no trees, metal roofs, no ventilation”. We never use words like “slum” or “encroachment”, which in Delhi can feed eviction narratives.
  • No caste, religion or similar breakdowns, even where data exists.
  • No photos with identifiable people or homes in the foreground. The tree, sky and building bars carry the story instead.
  • No death or illness numbers on the map. Official and NGO counts for Delhi differ widely and are disputed, and the data cannot support them by area.
  • No accounts, cookies or tracking of individual readers. We count only anonymous totals (visits, story completions, shares).
